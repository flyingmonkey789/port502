Open-Weight AI Models in Critical Infrastructure

Trends, opportunities, risks, and security implications

An assessment of the practical opportunities, risks, and sentiment around deploying locally hosted open-weight models within critical industries.

01

Executive summary

As critical industries undergo continued modernization efforts, the evaluation of secure artificial intelligence adoption is a growing concern. One of the leading routes for bringing AI securely into critical infrastructure is through the use of open-weight, locally hosted models.

Open-weight models are AI models whose trained parameters are published for anyone to download, so operators can run, inspect, and fine-tune them on their own hardware instead of relying on a vendor’s cloud.

Through the implementation of these models, teams unable or unwilling to send operational data to a public cloud can now run capable models on hardware they own, inside networks they control.

In 2026, deployments across electric power, nuclear, and manufacturing moved from trials and proofs of concept into daily use; however, nearly all of them stopped short of enabling autonomous action. The primary cited use case to date has been decision support: reading documents, explaining alarms, and assisting analysts rather than acting on physical processes.

Operators say the largest opportunity for open-weight models lies in work that cloud AI is not permitted to reach, such as air-gapped sites, regulated records, and security telemetry that cannot leave the plant. The biggest concern from end users is not misoperation or hallucination, since use cases are limited to advisory tasks, but rather that local AI introduces an asset type that teams do not yet know how to govern.

Security teams evaluating open-weight models should treat every locally hosted deployment as an OT-adjacent asset that is inventoried, sourced from a trusted publisher, segmented, monitored, and limited to advisory roles until controls mature.

  1. 01Local is the default, not the exception. Compliance rules and disconnected networks push critical infrastructure toward on-premises AI by design rather than preference.
  2. 02Value sits beside the control loop. Production use cases are read-only or advisory, with federal guidance steering operators to keep it that way.
  3. 03On-premises is not a security control. Self-hosting shifts risk from the vendor to the operator, who now owns a model supply chain and an inference stack few OT teams have defended before.

02

Trends

Across the broader market, open-weight adoption accelerated sharply in 2026: 42% of enterprises ran these models in production by September, up from 31% in July Futurum. Critical infrastructure follows a narrower, more cautious version of that curve, and adoption varies widely by sector.

42%of enterprises running open-weight models in production, Sept 2026Futurum
5%of OT security teams with agentic AI in productionNozomi / Takepoint
47%of production users call country of origin a major or deciding factorFuturum

Energy leads. EPRI’s Power Chat runs OpenAI’s open-weight gpt-oss-120b on a single deskside system and works on fully air-gapped networks NVIDIA. At Diablo Canyon, on-site generative AI searches a library of more than 9,000 operating procedures Utility Dive. For electric utilities, NERC CIP monitoring requirements make cloud AI connections an audit liability, which reinforces the on-premises path.

Manufacturing adopts through vendors. OT suppliers are packaging models into their products. Siemens’ Industrial Copilot for Operations runs at the edge on an industrial PC with no internet connection.

Water and transportation lag. Few public deployments exist in either sector. Where AI appears, it tends to arrive inside security tools rather than operator-built systems.

The use cases moving into production are document search, compliance evidence gathering, and operator assistance. Autonomous agents remain early: only 5% of OT security teams report agentic AI in production. Model origin is also tightening as a filter, with 47% of production users now calling country of origin a major or deciding factor.

03

Opportunities

Locally hosted models create the most value where data cannot leave, networks cannot reach, and a human stays in the loop.

Air-gapped analysis. Nuclear plants, substations, and pipeline control centers often have no outbound internet path. A local model brings search, summarization, and question answering to these sites for the first time, without opening a new connection to the outside world.

Maintenance and operator support. Models that explain error codes, surface the right procedure, and draft shift handovers shorten downtime and help close the gap left by retiring engineers. Running at the edge also removes latency and cloud dependency from the plant floor.

On-premises data handling. Compliance evidence, asset inventories, and OT security telemetry are among an operator’s most sensitive records. Local models can process them without third-party exposure, and defenders can tune them to answer the security questions that frontier cloud models sometimes refuse. Dragos reports that pairing AI with OT context cut assessments from two to three weeks to two to three days Dragos.

The economics also favor local deployment for routine, high-volume work, with no per-token bill and no vendor deprecations forcing a safety case to be re-validated.

04

Risks

The best-documented failures today are ordinary configuration and supply-chain problems, not sophisticated model sabotage.

~175Kpublicly reachable Ollama servers, Jan 2026SentinelOne / Censys
~45%of popular Hugging Face models still use picklearXiv
12xmore likely to follow hijacking instructions (DeepSeek-based agents)NIST

Exposed infrastructure. Researchers counted roughly 175,000 publicly reachable Ollama servers in January 2026, and nearly half had tool-calling enabled SentinelOne / Censys. A model built to run privately becomes an open API with a single misconfigured setting.

Model integrity and supply chain. About 45% of popular Hugging Face models still use pickle, a file format that can execute code when loaded arXiv. More subtle are hidden backdoors: research shows triggered behaviors can survive standard safety training Anthropic et al., and no available method can yet prove a model is clean.

Data leakage, real and perceived. Running a model locally does not send data back to its developer, so the leakage fear attached to foreign models is partly misplaced. The policy risk is real, though. In October 2026, DHS ordered federal civilian agencies to remove DeepSeek, including third-party products built on it.

Unsafe automation. Risk rises sharply once models can take actions. NIST testing found DeepSeek-based agents 12 times more likely than leading U.S. models to follow hijacking instructions HPCwire, and it describes hijacking resistance as unsolved across all frontier models.

Adversaries benefit as well. Guardrail-stripped variants of new open models appear within hours of release, and in a Mexican water utility intrusion, an AI model pointed an IT-only attacker toward the SCADA interface without being asked.

05

Security implications

The controls that protect OT today, including visibility, segmentation, and secure remote access, apply directly to local AI. What changes is that the model itself must now be managed as an asset. Dragos estimates that fewer than 10% of OT environments worldwide have visibility and monitoring in place, so a model inside an unmonitored network adds one more unmonitored system with an API.

Before deployment, operators should put five controls in place:

  1. 01GovernanceAssign an owner and a written purpose to every model, and record each one in the asset inventory.
  2. 02ProvenanceAccept only models from known publishers, in safe formats such as safetensors, with verified hashes. Record the publisher’s jurisdiction and treat it as a procurement criterion.
  3. 03SegmentationKeep inference servers off routable paths to the internet and outside control-system zones, with authentication in front of every endpoint.
  4. 04Bounded autonomyFollow CISA’s May 2026 guidance to begin with low-risk, non-sensitive use cases and avoid broad access to critical systems CDO Magazine.
  5. 05MonitoringLog prompts, tool calls, and model updates. Because no model can yet be proven clean, the defensible boundary is what it is permitted to do and what it actually did.

06

Practitioner sentiment

Across practitioners, vendors, and investors, the mood is a cautious yes. Each group wants AI in critical infrastructure, and each wants a human between the model and the physical process.

87.7%of OT security respondents using, evaluating, or planning AINozomi / Takepoint
7.9%have deployed AI across multiple functionsNozomi / Takepoint
10%of in-house open-weight builds report sustained ROI at scaleFuturum

Practitioners are experimenting, but few have scaled. In the 2026 Nozomi/Takepoint survey, 87.7% of OT security respondents were using, evaluating, or planning AI, but only 7.9% had deployed it across multiple functions Nozomi Networks. Among utilities, 41% cite cybersecurity as a top obstacle to AI adoption, yet 87% expect AI to strengthen their security posture Cisco. Nuclear operators draw the line explicitly: Atomic Canyon’s CEO has said he does not want AI running a nuclear plant “right now” CalMatters.

Vendors are concerned but measured. Dragos sees no evidence that AI will independently take down infrastructure. It does expect AI to draw more IT attackers into OT and to shrink exploit development from days toward minutes.

Investors are betting on infrastructure. Futurum’s data suggests most of the value is flowing to the vendors that sell compute and platforms, while in-house open-weight builds report sustained ROI at scale in only 10% of cases.

07

Methodology

This edition was built with the 502 Engine from 37 public sources published between January 2024 and October 2026. They fall into five groups: threat research from Dragos, ReversingLabs, SentinelOne, Censys, and Cisco; government guidance and evaluations from CISA, NSA, NIST CAISI, DHS, and the EU; analyst and market surveys from Futurum (N=200 enterprises), Nozomi Networks with Takepoint Research, Cisco (175+ utilities), and IBM; peer-reviewed and preprint research; and published deployment case studies.

Known limitations. Most survey data skews North American and is vendor-sponsored. Public deployment case studies favor announced successes, so failed pilots are underrepresented. Model capabilities and policy are both changing month to month, and all figures reflect conditions as of November 2026.
